# The order of new cards: research

In what order should words never studied be introduced. Today the deck order is alphabetical inside three tiers
(class words, textbook units 1-5, the rest); alphabetical order is a last resort. Researched 29.10.2026 for a learner who
studies Greek at B1 next to a weekly class. Not repeated here: interference and retrieval-induced forgetting
(`cross-deck-overlap.md` §2, Tinkham 1993, Waring 1997), open Greek frequency lists (`deck-formats-and-sources.md` §3.6),
the first showing of a card (`first-exposure-research.md`, §3 and §4).

Legend: **[S]** read in the source (abstract where noted), **[H]** checked on the host or page, **[I]** our inference,
**[U]** unverified.

## 1. Lexical sets: related words together or apart

New words related in meaning (coordinates, synonyms, antonyms: "personality adjectives") against unrelated words, and
against words of one scene or story ("thematic" sets).

| Source | Design | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Tinkham 1997, Second Language Research 13(2), via Nation 2000 | Pseudowords; semantic sets, thematic sets, unrelated; trials to criterion | 96 comparisons, semantic vs unrelated: unrelated faster in 80, equal in 13, semantic faster in 3. Thematic sets took 15% fewer repetitions than unrelated: faster in 47, equal in 29, slower in 20. Nation attributes part of the thematic gap to mixed parts of speech. **[S]** Nation 2000 text; Tinkham's paper not read **[U]** |
| Erten & Tekin 2008, System 36(3) | 60 fourth graders, 80 words, related vs unrelated sets, picture-word tests | Unrelated better; the ERIC abstract says the difference persisted in the long term and recall took longer for related items. **[S]** [ERIC EJ804977](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ804977) abstract. Li et al. 2026 describe the delayed gap as no longer significant **[S]**, so the delayed result is **[U]** |
| Papathanasiou 2009, ELT Journal 63(4) | Greek learners of English, beginners | Related clusters impede learning at beginner level. **[S]** search snippet of the OUP abstract; the page refused (403) |
| Bolger & Zapata 2011, Language Learning 61(2) | 32 new labels for known concepts in four categories, put into stories: one category per story or four per story | Slight advantage for the unrelated condition; story context "may have surmounted much of the disadvantage". **[S]** abstract (Semantic Scholar API) |
| Nakata & Suzuki 2019, SSLA 41(2) | 133 learners, 48 pairs, massed vs spaced, half related, half unrelated; immediate and one-week posttests | No significant related-unrelated difference on either posttest; related words produced more interference errors. Spacing helped both kinds at one week (unrelated d = 0.72, related d = 0.38), unrelated more. Delayed accuracy: massed 17.7% related vs 16.0% unrelated, spaced about 24% related vs 29.1% unrelated (p = .49 and .10). **[S]** full text |
| Li, Uchihara, Nakata & Murphy 2026, System 142, 104141 | Three-level meta-analysis, 30 studies | Trials to criterion: g = 1.02 [0.60, 1.44], 13 effects from 6 studies, related sets need more trials. Immediate posttest: g = 0.01 [-0.25, 0.26], 37 effects from 21 studies. Moderators: longer training time per session (F(1,30) = 4.33, p = .046) and words shown in context (g = 0.32 with context, -0.05 as word lists). **[S]** PDF read |

- **Nation 2000** (TESOL Journal 9(2), 6-10) reads the same literature as a warning. Guidelines: present the items at
  different times, the most useful first and the next "at least several days apart"; or use widely differing contexts and
  collocates so the words are not interchangeable in one frame. Formal similarity on top of related meaning (Tuesday,
  Thursday) makes it worse. Usefulness (frequency or need) and avoidance of interference "are more important than aiming
  for early completeness of lexical sets". **[S]** full text. He cites Schneider, Healy & Bourne 1998: related words were
  easier at first, unrelated ones easier on a long-term test and in relearning. **[S]** via Nation.
- **Delayed tests.** The meta-analysis pools immediate posttests and has no delayed-posttest synthesis; its text
  mentions delayed tests only in the summary of Erten & Tekin. **[S]** Delayed evidence is Nakata & Suzuki (one week, no
  reliable gap) and Erten & Tekin (contested above).
- **Does it hold with spacing?** In Nakata & Suzuki the gap related-unrelated stayed non-significant in both massed and
  spaced conditions. The hypothesis that spacing rescues related items failed: spacing helped unrelated items more. **[S]**
- **Thematic sets** carry the best news in this section (Tinkham 1997, 15% fewer repetitions), measured on pseudowords
  with trials to criterion, so no retention figure exists. **[S]** via Nation.
- Pooled across designs the cost of a semantic cluster sits in the learning phase (g about 1.0) and mostly disappears
  by the posttest (g about 0). A learner who studies with spaced retrieval pays the cost as extra Again answers early
  and lower first-session accuracy. **[I]**

## 2. Word families: δύναμη / δυναμώνω / δυναμικός

| Source | What it shows |
|---|---|
| Bauer & Nation 1993, Int. J. Lexicography 6(4) | A word family is defined by frequency, regularity, productivity and predictability of affixes; English affixes are graded into seven levels. **[S]** abstract (Semantic Scholar API) |
| Schmitt & Zimmerman 2002, TESOL Quarterly 36(2) | 106 learners had to produce the noun, verb, adjective and adverb forms of 16 words; learners know some members of a family and not the rest. **[S]** abstract text on a Scribd copy and the ResearchGate snippet ("partial productive knowledge"). A 58.8% mean seen in a citing paper is second-hand **[U]** |
| Sasao & Webb 2017, Language Teaching Research 21(1) | Word Part Levels Test: 118 affixes, 417 and 1,348 test takers, difficulty levels by Rasch analysis. A test of affix knowledge; no teaching comparison. **[S]** ERIC abstract |
| Laufer 2023, SSLA | Learners given the base word and sentence context understood many derived words they failed in isolation (grade 8: 35% to 68%); the 12th graders understood 74% of the frequent affixes. Argues that complete receptive morphological knowledge is not needed in context. **[S]** open-access text, read in part |
| Li & Behzadnia 2025, SSLA (online 15.12.2025) | Chinese learners of English learned novel two-part words with pictures. Training a stem helped later items containing it; the training effect was larger for items in large morphological families than in small ones; effects were smaller in L2 than in L1. **[S]** abstract |
| Family-size effect, Baayen et al. 1997, Bertram et al. 2000, Mulder et al. 2014 | Words with large morphological families are recognised faster and more accurately, in L1 and L2. **[S]** via the review paragraph in Li & Behzadnia; originals not read **[U]** |

- **Together or apart.** No study was found that teaches derivatives of one root together versus on separate days to
  adult L2 learners. The nearest results point one way: related forms help each other in processing (family size) and in
  learning novel complex words (stem training, larger effect in larger families), and learners hold partial derivative
  knowledge (Schmitt & Zimmerman), so derivatives are not learned for free from the base. **[I]**
- **Interference.** The interfering relations in the literature are coordinates, synonyms, antonyms and formally similar
  words that can fill the same slot (Nation 2000). Members of one root differ in part of speech and so rarely fill one
  slot; that is a reading of Nation, and no experiment tests it. **[I]**
- **Instruction research** found by search is L1 primary-school work (Educational Psychology Review 2024 meta-analysis of
  morphology instruction, [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-024-09953-3) **[S]** abstract); it
  does not transfer to an adult learner of a new language. **[I]**
- **Greek.** No English-language study of Greek derivational morphology teaching was found (see the list at the end).

## 3. Frequency order

- **Coverage.** In Nation 2006 (Canadian Modern Language Review, BNC-based lists, Table 3 and conclusions) the first 1,000
  families plus proper nouns cover 78-81% of written text and about 85% of spoken text; the fourth and fifth 1,000
  give about 3% of written text together, the sixth to ninth about 2%, the tenth to fourteenth under 1%. 98% coverage
  needs 8,000-9,000 families for written and 6,000-7,000 for spoken text. **[S]** PDF read. The figures are English;
  a Greek equivalent was not found **[U]**.
- **Reading of the curve.** Frequency order pays most in the first 1-2 thousand words and flattens after; a B1 learner's
  unknown words sit on the flat part. **[I]**
- **Course follower.** Nation names two criteria that outrank completing a set: usefulness, which he writes as "frequency
  or need", and avoiding interference. A word the class uses this week is "need". **[S]** Nation 2000. No study was
  found that compares a frequency-ordered list with a course-ordered one for a learner who attends a course. **[U]**
- **Greek sources.** FrequencyWords, KELLY (CEFR level and ipm), Leipzig and wordfreq: `deck-formats-and-sources.md` §3.6.
  The KELLY list tags CEFR levels, which makes "B1 words first" a key that exists. **[S]** that file; list content
  not re-checked.

## 4. Course recency: when to practise a word met in class

- **Optimal gap.** Cepeda, Vul, Rohrer, Wixted & Pashler 2008 (Psychological Science 19, 1095-1102): retention intervals up
  to a year; at each interval performance rose and then fell with the gap, and the best gap was about 20% of the
  test delay. **[S]** abstract via the author's page; a 5-10% figure for a one-year delay is second-hand **[U]**.
  For a one-week horizon that is one to two days. **[I]**
- **Vocabulary in schools.** Küpper-Tetzel, Erdfelder & Dickhäuser 2014 (Instructional Science 42): with a 7-day test
  relearning after 1 day gave the best result; with a 35-day test lags of both 1 and 10 days helped. **[S]** search
  snippet of the abstract; the page refused (403).
- **First retrieval.** Karpicke & Roediger 2007 (JEP:LMC 33(4)): vocabulary pairs; expanding schedules with an immediate
  first test gave a short-term benefit, equally spaced retrieval gave better long-term retention; the abstract names
  delaying the first retrieval, which makes it harder, as the important factor. **[S]** snippet and TLDR of the abstract.
  Nakata 2015 (SSLA 37) found no reliable advantage for expanding over equal spacing in L2 vocabulary. **[S]** snippet,
  secondhand **[U]**.
- **Curriculum-synchronised review.** No study of spaced-repetition review tied to a course syllabus was found; the pages
  returned were product reviews. **[U]**
- **Reading.** Nothing found compares "soon after the lesson" with "later" for words met in class. The lag studies
  favour a first retrieval about a day after the lesson when the next test is a week away, and say nothing against
  the same-day learning steps that `first-exposure-research.md` §4 already covers. **[I]**

- The tools use five keys: curriculum position (WaniKani, Duolingo, Bunpro textbook paths), frequency or level
  (Clozemaster, Bunpro, AnkiMorphs), the learner's own need (LingQ, the Anki frequency-deck workflow), priority
  (SuperMemo), and random. Anki offers position and random. No page read describes a rule that spaces
  semantically related words apart; Anki's sibling burying spaces cards of one note only. **[I]** from the pages above.

## Reading for Mnemit [I]

1. **Key order for a new card.** Tier first (class words, textbook units, the rest) as today. Inside a tier the
   evidence gives no reason to prefer alphabetical. Use course position for class words (newest lesson first, see 3)
   and frequency or CEFR level for the other tiers, with the alphabet only as the final tie-break. Nation's "frequency
   or need" supports need above frequency.
2. **Frequency is a tie-break at B1.** The coverage curve flattens after the first two or three thousand families
   (Nation 2006), and a B1 deck's unknown words sit on the flat part. A frequency key inside the unit and rest tiers
   costs little and is right on average; it is no reason to reorder tiers.
3. **Class words soon after the lesson.** The lag studies (Cepeda 2008, Küpper-Tetzel 2014) favour about a day
   between the lesson and the first retrieval when the next test is a week away. Newest lesson first fits; older
   lessons fall back to frequency. No study compares this with a later start for words met in class.
4. **Lexical sets: spread, do not forbid.** A semantic cluster raises early difficulty (g about 1.0 in the learning
   phase) and leaves retention close to equal (g about 0 on immediate tests; one delayed study, no reliable gap).
   Cap the number of new cards of one set per day (Nation: several days apart; two or three of a set per day as a
   starting value is arbitrary), interleave sets in the day's queue, and leave scene-based groups (thematic sets,
   15% fewer repetitions in Tinkham 1997) and cards with an example sentence alone: context was the one moderator that
   turned the cost into a benefit (g = 0.32).
5. **Families: base first, derivatives follow on their own.** The evidence is for help between family members and for
   partial derivative knowledge, with no sign of interference between root relatives. Order the base word before its
   derivatives inside a tier; the derivatives are ordinary new cards and the evidence asks for no gap. Whether a
   derivative shown in the same sitting is answered through its base, inflating the grade the way a sibling does
   (`production-cards-research.md`, `cross-deck-overlap.md` §2), is untested **[U]**.
6. **Formal look-alikes are the exception.** Words that share a spelling or differ by one syllable and also sit near in
   meaning (Nation's Tuesday/Thursday case) are the pairs the interference literature predicts; keep those a few days
   apart, and ahead of everything else when a sibling-like pair is found.
7. **Expose the key, not a mode.** Anki offers gather and sort orders and users still build workflows around them
   (frequency deck, random insertion, reposition). A single stored sort key per tier (position, frequency, CEFR level,
   alphabet) with an easy override per card is the smaller feature; a random order has no support in the evidence for a
   course follower.
